INTRODUCTION

What does it mean to connect?

Part 02 travels from the birth of electronic computing to the smartphone age. Its subject is not simply a collection of devices, but a transformation in the space between people.

The disappearance of distance—and the scarcity of attention

As the Industrial Revolution used machinery to exceed the limits of the body, the Digital Revolution used computation and networks to exceed the limits of distance. A message that once took days by post, or hours by telegraph, can now cross the planet in milliseconds. People on opposite sides of the world can speak as though they shared a room.

But when distance ceased to be the main constraint, another scarcity became visible. In 1971, Herbert A. Simon observed that an abundance of information consumes attention. Today, messages, alerts, feeds, and recommendations compete continuously for a finite human capacity: the ability to notice, remain present, and think deeply.

This is the paradox at the heart of Part 02. Digital technology was built to connect people. It also fragments attention and helps create a distinctly modern experience: being continuously connected while feeling alone. The question is no longer only whether a tool works, but whether we direct the tool—or allow its design to direct us.

The middle movement of a trilogy

Parts 01 through 03 read technological history as three linked revolutions. Part 01 explored the Industrial Revolution, which extended the body and released large-scale production from muscular power. Part 02 explores the Information Revolution, which extends intelligence and relationships by widening access to information. Part 03 turns to the AI Revolution, in which machines begin to extend—or substitute for—parts of thinking itself.

Every revolution carries a double movement. Industrial machinery expanded production while weakening forms of craft autonomy. Digital networks democratized access and expression while eroding concentration and time with the people physically before us. Generative AI may release us from some cognitive labor—but what might it cost?

The Industrial Revolution liberated muscle. The Digital Revolution liberated information. The AI Revolution may liberate thought. Each asks the same question: what did freedom make possible, and what did it make easier to lose?

HISTORICAL TIMELINE

From computation to constant connection

The Digital Revolution unfolded through conceptual breakthroughs, new components, open standards, commercial platforms, and changes in everyday behavior.

1936

Turing defines a universal machine

Alan Turing formalized the idea of computation through an imaginary machine that reads and writes symbols on a tape. The universal version could simulate any other such machine, establishing a theoretical foundation for the programmable computer—and for the later question, “Can machines think?”

Theory of computationUniversality
1945–1946

ENIAC and electronic computing

Built at the University of Pennsylvania, ENIAC weighed about 27 metric tons and used 17,468 vacuum tubes. Designed initially for artillery calculations, it demonstrated the speed and flexibility of large-scale electronic computation. Stored-program designs developed around the same period became the basis of modern computer architecture.

Electronic computerVacuum tubes
1947–1958

From the transistor to the integrated circuit

The transistor demonstrated at Bell Labs replaced bulky, power-hungry vacuum tubes with a much smaller solid-state switch. Integrated circuits then placed multiple components on a single semiconductor. Miniaturization made computing cheaper, more reliable, and eventually personal.

SemiconductorsMiniaturization
1969

ARPANET links distant computers

The first ARPANET nodes connected research institutions in the United States. Its packet-switching architecture allowed computers to share resources across a distributed network. Contrary to a common myth, ARPANET was not created solely to survive nuclear war; resilience was one influence within a broader research program.

Packet switchingDistributed network
1976–1981

The computer becomes personal

Apple I, Apple II, and IBM’s Personal Computer helped move computing from institutions into homes and offices. The decisive change was cultural as well as technical: an individual could now own a programmable machine and use it as a medium for work, play, and expression.

Personal computerIndividual agency
1989–1993

The World Wide Web opens

At CERN, Tim Berners-Lee proposed a system of linked documents built from URLs, HTTP, and HTML. In 1993, CERN released the Web’s core software into the public domain. That commitment to openness enabled people everywhere to build on a shared information space.

World Wide WebOpen standards
1998

Google maps an expanding web

Google’s PageRank treated links between pages as signals of relevance and authority. Search became the practical map of a rapidly growing information world. Finding knowledge increasingly began with a search box rather than a library shelf.

SearchAccess to knowledge
2004–2006

Social media turns audiences into publishers

Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter helped move publication from media institutions toward individuals. This widened participation and enabled new forms of witness and community. It also accelerated misinformation, filter bubbles, harassment, and political polarization.

Participatory mediaAlgorithmic feeds
2007

The smartphone makes computing ambient

The iPhone combined a phone, media player, networked computer, sensors, and a touch interface. Computing became something people carried rather than visited. The boundary between “online” and “offline” began to dissolve.

SmartphoneAlways connected
2020s

Digital infrastructure becomes a lifeline

During COVID-19, remote work, online classes, telemedicine, and video calls shifted from optional conveniences to social infrastructure. Zoom reported growth from roughly ten million daily meeting participants in December 2019 to hundreds of millions by spring 2020. People also rediscovered what screens do not easily carry: touch, atmosphere, informal encounter, and embodied presence.

Remote presenceDigital dependence

THEMES IN DEPTH

Four transformations of the connected world

Computation became personal, knowledge became linked, connection became constant, and attention became an industry.

THEME 01

The birth of the computer

From a room-sized calculator to a responsive medium

ENIAC was a room-sized machine whose 17,468 vacuum tubes generated enormous heat. Reconfiguring it meant moving cables and switches by hand. By contemporary standards its power was tiny, yet it established a new possibility: symbols could be transformed by electronic rules at unprecedented speed.

Turing supplied the theoretical idea of universal computation. John von Neumann and his collaborators helped articulate a stored-program architecture in which instructions and data occupy the same memory. Most general-purpose computers still inherit this basic logic.

The personal-computer movement changed the machine’s social meaning. When a person typed a command and watched it respond, calculation became dialogue. The computer emerged not only as a tool for answers, but as a medium for making worlds.

THEME 02

The internet revolution

The world became a web, and “knowing” changed

Berners-Lee invented the Web to help researchers share information across incompatible systems. Its power came from a simple grammar: identify a resource, request it through a common protocol, and link it to another document. CERN’s decision to make the technology openly available allowed the Web to become shared infrastructure rather than a private product.

Google gave the expanding Web a navigable surface. Search changed the everyday meaning of knowledge: remembering an answer became less important than knowing how to find, judge, and connect information.

Social media removed many traditional gatekeepers. Anyone could publish, organize, document injustice, teach, or find community. But gatekeeping did not disappear; it changed form. Rankings, recommendation engines, platform rules, and business incentives became new gatekeepers—powerful precisely because they were often invisible.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhen everyone can publish, who decides what becomes visible—and what responsibilities belong to the reader?

THEME 03

The smartphone and an accelerating world

“Anytime, anywhere” became the default

The smartphone did more than combine existing devices. The touch interface made computation feel direct, while cameras, location, motion sensors, and constant networking made the device aware of its surroundings. Technology became transparent: people could enter the digital world without feeling that they were operating a computer.

Before smartphones, going online was often a distinct activity. After them, connection became a continuous condition. This created extraordinary convenience and accessibility, but it also made disconnection something that had to be defended. The “right to disconnect” emerged because the boundary between personal time and network time no longer held by itself.

The pandemic demonstrated both sides of digital presence. Networks sustained work, schooling, medicine, friendship, and family contact. They also showed that transmission is not the same as presence. A corridor encounter, the mood of a classroom, a hand on a shoulder, and shared silence contain information that video compresses or loses.

THEME 04

The light and shadow of digital life

Attention, loneliness, and the architecture of choice

Design ethicist Tristan Harris has compared the smartphone to a slot machine: every refresh may deliver a rewarding surprise. Likes, streaks, autoplay, infinite scroll, and push notifications are not neutral ornaments. Many are optimized to increase time and return visits because human attention can be converted into advertising revenue.

Popular claims that modern attention has fallen to “eight seconds” are not supported by a dependable universal measure. The deeper concern is nevertheless real: frequent interruption and task-switching impose cognitive costs, while environments organized around novelty make sustained reading and reflection harder to protect. The problem is not that the human mind suddenly changed, but that its environment did.

At the story’s end, Haru sets down the smartphone and opens a book. This is not a rejection of digital life. It declares that distance from a tool can itself be chosen. Agency begins when defaults become visible: when we can ask why we opened the device, whose goal an interface serves, and whether this moment calls for connection or quiet.

CORE ARGUMENT

The Digital Revolution as a double-edged connection

The last eighty years tell two inseparable stories: humanity acquired its most powerful instruments of connection, and those instruments reorganized attention, intimacy, and public reality.

I. The paradox of connection

In Alone Together (2011), sociologist Sherry Turkle describes a central contradiction of digital life: people can maintain more contacts than ever while feeling that conversation has become thinner. Connection is countable—followers, messages, reactions—but closeness also depends on qualities that resist counting: undivided attention, mutual risk, shared silence, and the ability to remain with another person when nothing entertaining happens.

This is also a question of design. Platforms optimize for continued connection because continued connection supports their business. Human relationships, however, also need unconnected time: solitude, distance, forgetting, and the freedom not to answer immediately. Read receipts and presence indicators can turn response into obligation. A technical affordance becomes a social norm.

II. Liberation and loss

The Digital Revolution widened access to knowledge, communication, and publication. A person no longer needs to live near a great library, control a printing press, or receive permission from a broadcaster to learn or speak publicly. These gains are profound, especially for people separated from resources or community by geography, disability, language, or social position.

What became harder to protect was concentrated attention and presence with the person directly before us. The issue is not a mythical universal attention span, but an ecosystem of interruption. Notifications divide thought; endless feeds remove stopping cues; recommendation systems make “the next thing” effortless. Attention does not disappear—it is continually redirected.

If information is abundant, attention becomes the scarce resource. The central question is not only what we can access, but what deserves the hours of a human life.

III. The new gatekeepers

The Web was celebrated as an escape from old media gatekeepers. Yet a world without editors did not become a world without selection. Search ranking, feed algorithms, moderation rules, social graphs, and advertising auctions decide which pieces of reality rise into view. These systems can help people navigate abundance, but their criteria are rarely identical to truth, public value, or human flourishing.

A democratic information environment therefore requires more than freedom to post. It requires transparency, media literacy, accountable institutions, and the capacity to encounter views outside one’s algorithmic profile. The task is not to restore a single gatekeeper, but to make the new ones visible and contestable.

IV. The bridge to the AI Revolution

Part 02 ends with a person choosing the distance between self and device. That act becomes more urgent in Part 03. Generative AI moves beyond retrieving existing information: it can compose an answer, image, plan, or judgment. When machines do not merely find responses but produce them, what capacities should human beings strengthen?

Part 01 asked about the extension of the body. Part 02 asks about the extension of relationships. Part 03 asks about the extension of thought. The lesson of the connected world is not “reject the tool,” but “do not surrender the right to choose the relationship.”

PART 02 WITHIN THE TRILOGY

Part 01 revealed how technology reorganized bodies, labor, and time. Part 02 shows networks reorganizing attention and relationships. Together they prepare the deepest question of Part 03: when a tool enters the act of thinking, where should human agency live?

THE CENTRAL QUESTION OF PART 02Is being connected the same as being close?

Digital technology can erase distance without creating understanding. A screen can carry a voice, image, and message—but human closeness also asks for attention, interpretation, responsibility, and presence.

GLOSSARY

Words that reveal the network

Each term names both a technical development and a change in how people know, meet, and choose.

ENIAC

The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, completed at the University of Pennsylvania in 1945 and publicly unveiled in 1946. It was one of the earliest general-purpose electronic digital computers, built with 17,468 vacuum tubes and initially used for military calculations.

Its importance lies less in comparison with today’s devices than in proving that large-scale electronic computation was practical.

Personal computer · PC

A general-purpose computer designed for individual use. Machines such as Apple II and IBM PC helped move computing out of specialized institutions and into homes, schools, and offices.

“Personal” described a redistribution of power: individuals could own and program the machine themselves.

Internet

The global system that connects computer networks through shared protocols, especially TCP/IP. Its roots include ARPANET and many other research networks. The internet is the infrastructure; the World Wide Web is one service built on top of it.

World Wide Web · WWW

A system of interlinked resources accessed through web browsers, developed by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN. URLs identify resources, HTTP transfers them, and HTML gives pages structure. The Web’s open standards allowed it to grow without a single central owner.

Search engine

A system that crawls, indexes, and ranks information from the Web in response to a query. Search engines do not simply find information; their ranking choices shape what becomes visible and credible.

Search retrieves existing material. Generative AI composes a new response from learned patterns—an important distinction for Part 03.

Social media

Platforms that allow people to create, share, and interact with user-generated content. They lower the barriers to publishing and community formation, while their ranking systems and incentives influence attention, speech, and public life.

Attention economy

An economy in which human attention is scarce and therefore commercially valuable. The phrase builds on Herbert A. Simon’s observation that abundant information consumes attention. Many digital services exchange free access for data and time that can be sold to advertisers.

Filter bubble

Eli Pariser’s term for the personalized environment produced when systems predict what a user is likely to prefer. Personalization can be useful, but it can reduce unexpected encounters and conceal how different another person’s information world has become.

An echo chamber is related but distinct: beliefs are repeated and reinforced within a like-minded social group.

Digital detox · Right to disconnect

A digital detox is a deliberate period away from devices or services. The right to disconnect is a labor principle protecting workers from expectations of constant availability outside working hours. Both turn disconnection from an accident into a choice.

A MAP OF QUESTIONS

Continue the inquiry

Today, how many times did you open your phone intentionally—and how many times did a notification or habit open it for you?

Compare memory with your screen-time record. The gap may reveal which choices have become automatic.

Have you ever felt “alone together”—surrounded by digital contact but missing closeness?

What was absent: physical presence, attention, vulnerability, shared time, or something else?

If the internet stopped for one week, what would become impossible? What might become unexpectedly easier?

Separate genuine infrastructure from habitual convenience.

What did the open release of the Web make possible? When should knowledge infrastructure be public, and when is private ownership justified?

Consider the tension between open standards, innovation, maintenance, and intellectual property.
