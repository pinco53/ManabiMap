INTRODUCTION

Why begin with the Industrial Revolution?

Haru and Grandpa is a story about AI and human beings. Yet Part 01 begins not with AI or computers, but with the steam engine—in the factory towns of 1760s England, amid coal smoke and the sound of iron. Why?

The starting point of a trilogy

Parts 01 through 03 form a trilogy. They trace the changing relationship between humans and machines through three technological revolutions: the Industrial Revolution, the Digital Revolution, and the AI Revolution. Part 01 is the foundation and point of departure.

There is a reason to begin here. To understand AI, we first need an archetype for what it means when technology transforms society. The steam engine was the first technology in human history to replace muscular labor on a large scale. It was more than an improved tool. It forced people to reconsider the meaning of work, the experience of time, the purpose of education, and human dignity itself.

The question AI presents to us today—what is the value of a human being when machines can replace human work?—was asked in the same structural form 250 years ago. Knowing that history is the shortest path to understanding where we stand now.

Reading the recurring pattern of technological revolutions

In The Great Transformation (1944), historian and political economist Karl Polanyi argued that the Industrial Revolution changed far more than production methods. It transformed the self-regulating mechanisms of society. Land, labor, and money were drawn into markets as commodities, and more and more human activity came to be measured by “efficiency.”

The same pattern has returned in later revolutions. The Digital Revolution made information into a commodity. The AI Revolution is beginning to commodify judgment itself. Part 01 follows the Industrial Revolution carefully so that we can first absorb the original form of this pattern. Once we recognize the pattern, we are better able to see the structure inside new change.

Every technological revolution restates the same question in a new key: What became free, and what was lost? The Industrial Revolution is the opening movement.

The story travels roughly fifty years, from rural England in the 1760s to the industrial cities of the early nineteenth century. It begins with the quiet rhythm of the countryside, then follows the birth of steam power, the rise of factories, the building of railways, and the emergence of urban life governed by the clock.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE

From the 1700s into the 1800s

The Industrial Revolution was not a single event. It was a chain of gradual changes unfolding over many decades.

Before the 1760s

Agrarian England

Most people lived in rural communities. Seasons, sunrise, and sunset set the rhythm of life. Handcraft and domestic production remained central, and many workers controlled their own pace. Productivity was low, but individual discretion was comparatively high. This was the world before the division of labor described by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776).

Agrarian societyDomestic production
1712

Newcomen’s atmospheric engine

Thomas Newcomen developed an early steam engine to pump water from coal mines. Its thermal efficiency was extremely low—around one percent—but it demonstrated that steam could power machinery. James Watt would transform the design more than fifty years later.

Steam engineCoal mining
1769

James Watt’s improved steam engine

Watt patented a separate condenser that dramatically improved efficiency. A machine once used mainly for pumping water became a general-purpose engine. This was a decisive step away from humanity’s dependence on muscle power.

Separate condenserTurning point
1770s–1780s

Spinning machines and the factory system

The spinning jenny (1764), water frame (1769), and spinning mule (1779) rapidly mechanized textiles. Richard Arkwright’s water-powered mills helped establish the modern factory: workers began moving from household workshops into centralized workplaces built around machines.

Factory systemTextiles
Around 1800

The expansion of factory and child labor

As steam spread, factories no longer had to stand beside fast-flowing rivers. In textile mills, some children began work at five and labored for fourteen hours or more. Human time and bodies were increasingly calculated as costs, as labor power itself became a commodity.

Child laborCommodified labor
1825

The first public steam railway

The Stockton and Darlington Railway carried freight and passengers using locomotives associated with George Stephenson. In 1830, the Liverpool and Manchester Railway connected major cities by timetable. Physical distance began to be translated into units of time.

Steam locomotiveRailway age
From the 1840s

Standard time and mass education

Timetables demanded coordination, pushing Britain’s diverse local times toward “railway time.” As E. P. Thompson later showed, workers moved from the rhythm of tasks to the discipline of the clock. During the same century, elementary schooling expanded and helped normalize punctuality, schedules, and obedience to instructions.

THEMES IN DEPTH

What the Age of Steam changed

Five lenses reveal how a new source of power reordered work, travel, time, education, and the meaning of progress.

THEME 01

The birth of the steam engine

The moment humanity escaped the constraint of muscle power

James Watt’s improved steam engine (1769) was the first broadly useful general-purpose power system in human history. Until then, usable power came from human beings, animals, water, or wind. Each was tightly bound to place and condition: waterwheels needed rivers, and windmills needed wind. With coal and water, steam could produce steady power in many places and at many times.

The core of this transition was liberation from constraint. Steam lifted weights that muscles could not and spun thread at speeds hands could not reach. When Adam Smith described the productivity of division of labor, he still assumed the limits of the human body. Steam rewrote the assumption itself. Yet liberation always arrives with a cost—and that tension runs through the whole map.

THEME 02

The factory and the transformation of labor

The day a human being became a “part” of a machine

The factory system changed the meaning of work at its roots. Under domestic production, craftspeople often managed the whole process—from obtaining materials to delivering a finished product. They could decide how long to spend weaving a garment. The problem Karl Marx later called alienation begins with the loss of this relationship to the whole.

In factories, workers repeated only one fragment: spinning thread, replacing bobbins, watching a machine. The overall product disappeared from view. In Capital (1867), Marx described the factory worker as an “appendage of the machine.” This was not merely metaphorical. A five-year-old crawling beneath a spinning machine to retie broken thread had to fit a human body to the rhythm of machinery.

The cruelty of child labor was more than long hours and low wages. Children lost the time to discover the world at their own pace—childhood itself. The Factory Act of 1833 finally prohibited factory work for children under nine and limited hours for older children, though conditions remained severe.

THEME 03

The world connected by rail

Distance became “time,” and the world contracted

When the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830, people experienced speeds near fifty kilometers per hour for the first time—three or four times faster than a stagecoach. In The Railway Journey (1977), historian Wolfgang Schivelbusch analyzed the transformation of perception. Scenery became a moving panorama. Travel became transport: the middle of the journey faded, leaving departure and arrival.

The railway’s deepest change, however, was not speed but the unification of time. Before rail, British towns kept local solar time; London and Bristol differed by roughly ten minutes. Timetables could not tolerate that difference. By 1847, most railway companies had adopted Greenwich time, and national time gradually followed. Living by the same time was both a technological byproduct and a foundation of modern society.

THEME 04

Clocks and the standardization of education

How society produced “punctual people”

E. P. Thompson’s landmark essay “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism” (1967) showed how industrialization rebuilt the human experience of time. Before the factory, labor was often task-oriented. A field was plowed not “from this hour to that hour,” but until the field was finished. The task shaped the time.

The factory reversed that relationship. The hours came first, and work was assigned inside them. Thompson called this time-discipline. Factory clocks managed behavior minute by minute. Lateness brought fines; leaving early meant lost pay. Human time became a unit exchangeable for money.

Modern schooling helped embed this discipline. Britain’s 1870 Education Act expanded the institutional foundations of elementary schooling; compulsory attendance was strengthened in the following decade. Beyond curriculum, the timetable itself mattered: arrive at eight, study a prescribed subject for a prescribed period, move when the bell rings. Historians have compared this to a rehearsal for factory life. School taught knowledge, but it also trained bodies to obey the clock.

THEME 05

The price of convenience

Muscle was liberated, but individual rhythm was lost

The final theme is the paradox of the Industrial Revolution. Steam freed people from some forms of muscular labor. Railways expanded freedom of movement. Factories enabled mass production and put clothing and household goods once considered luxuries within ordinary reach. Material abundance undeniably grew.

At the same time, many people lost something previously taken for granted: living at their own pace. Waking with dawn, changing work with the seasons, resting when tired—the “individual rhythm” inside an inefficient agrarian world became standardized by clocks and machines. In Oliver Twist (1838) and Hard Times (1854), Charles Dickens portrayed human dignity crushed by this standardization.

This does not mean that progress was simply bad. The task is to account accurately for both gain and loss. Without that accounting, we are left with only two positions: celebrate technology or fear it. Part 01 asks us to stand in the space between them—the space of inquiry.

CORE ARGUMENT

The Industrial Revolution as a pattern

The Industrial Revolution matters not only as a historical episode, but as a template for reading every technological revolution that followed.

I. Liberation and alienation arrive together

The Industrial Revolution’s most important lesson is that liberation and alienation can be two sides of the same change. Steam released human beings from muscular labor, yet this very release helped turn workers into dependents of machinery. Marx’s insight was that the problem was not technology by itself, but the social relationships into which technology was placed.

The pattern returned in the Digital Revolution. The internet liberated access to information while producing around-the-clock overload and the extraction of attention. The AI Revolution may follow the same form. As judgment and creativity are “liberated,” what might become alienated? That question leads toward Part 03.

Technology is not neutral. But “Is it good or bad?” must always become a second question: Within what social relationships is it being used?

II. Redefining time—the legacy of E. P. Thompson

Thompson’s theory of time-discipline reinterpreted industrialization as a revolution in the experience of time. Before industry, time was something that flowed. Day length changed with the seasons; work density changed with weather and harvest. After industrialization, time became something measured: homogeneous, divisible, and tradable.

The change was difficult to reverse. Once a society internalized clock time, it could not easily return to task-oriented time. Modern people feel that they “do not have enough time” not because time itself diminished, but because industrial society installed a framework in which time is experienced as something consumed. This is an example of what sociologist Pierre Bourdieu called habitus: social structure embodied as disposition.

Smartphones make this management of time more precise. Every notification that interrupts attention is a refined descendant of the factory bell dividing workers’ behavior. Here we can already see the origins of the attention economy explored in Part 02.

III. Education’s enduring factory model

The basic structure of education designed in the industrial era has changed surprisingly little: whole-class teaching, timetables, periodic tests, age grades. These systems can be read as applications of a factory logic that sought people able to work at a common rhythm and meet standardized expectations. Education critic Ken Robinson repeatedly argued that modern schooling still carries the assumptions of industrialization.

In the AI age, this pattern is reaching a threshold. When AI can perform routine knowledge processing, the relative value of punctuality, compliance, and accurate answers to assigned problems begins to change. The history of educational standardization in Part 01 is therefore a necessary background for Part 03’s question: what should learning become?

PART 01 WITHIN THE TRILOGY

Part 01 depicts a past age, but its deeper purpose is to establish the pattern: liberation and alienation together; time redefined; education shaped by factory logic. Part 02 and Part 03 repeat these structures in new forms.

Part 01 liberated muscle. Part 02 liberates information processing. Part 03 liberates judgment and creation. In every age, what became free, and what was lost? Holding that question is the stance this trilogy asks of us.

GLOSSARY

Words that reveal the structure

These are more than definitions. Each term provides a lens for seeing why the history matters.

Industrial Revolution

The historical transformation, beginning in late-eighteenth-century Britain, from a society centered on agriculture and handcraft to one centered on industry and machines. It included not only steam engines and spinning machinery, but factories, urbanization, and new educational institutions. Although called a “revolution,” it accumulated gradually across decades.

Why it matters: it is the archetype for later technological revolutions, including AI.

Steam engine

A machine that heats water and uses expanding steam to produce motion. Newcomen developed an early practical engine in 1712. Watt’s separate condenser, patented in 1769, dramatically improved efficiency and helped turn the engine from a mine pump into a general source of power for factories, locomotives, and ships.

Watt did not invent steam power from nothing; an improvement to an existing system helped trigger a revolution.

Alienation · Entfremdung

Marx’s term for the separation of workers from the products they make, the process of making them, other workers, and their own human potential. When a person performs only one fragment of a process, the meaning and purpose of the whole can disappear.

A modern worker who cannot see the meaning of their job may face a structurally similar problem.

Time-discipline

E. P. Thompson’s term for the transition from task-oriented time to clock time under industrial capitalism. Work came to be valued not only by what was completed, but by measured hours spent.

Source: E. P. Thompson, “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism” (1967).

Factory Acts

A series of nineteenth-century British laws regulating factory labor. The 1833 Act prohibited factory work for children under nine and limited hours for older children. Though inadequate, it established the principle that the state could intervene in working conditions.

Domestic system · Putting-out system

A form of production that preceded factories. Merchants distributed materials to households, where craftspeople processed them and returned finished goods. Productivity was limited, but workers often retained more autonomy and combined craft with agricultural work.

Enclosure

The privatization and consolidation of common land in Britain from the sixteenth through nineteenth centuries. Rural people lost access to shared pastures and forests; many moved toward cities and became industrial labor. Enclosure helped create the human conditions for industrialization.

For Polanyi, enclosure anticipated the commodification of land, labor, and money at the center of market society.

Division of labor

Adam Smith’s famous pin-factory example in The Wealth of Nations showed how dividing work into specialized steps could transform productivity. Smith also warned that extreme specialization could narrow workers’ capacities and understanding.

A MAP OF QUESTIONS

Continue the inquiry

The Industrial Revolution liberated human beings from muscular labor. What might the Digital Revolution and AI Revolution liberate us from—and what might each cause us to lose?

Think of liberation and loss as a pair. This question bridges Part 01 to Parts 02 and 03.

Where does E. P. Thompson’s “time-discipline” remain today? Compare factory clocks with smartphone notifications, calendar apps, and remote-work status indicators.

Ask: who is managing whose time?

What remains of the factory model in schools? In the AI age, what could replace whole-class teaching divided by a fixed timetable?

Imagine combining task-oriented learning from before industrialization with contemporary technology.

Are the things lost as the price of convenience truly unrecoverable—or do they need to be recovered at all? What could “individual rhythm” mean now?

Consider not a return to the past, but reinvention in a new form.
