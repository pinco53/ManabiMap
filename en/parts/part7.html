INTRODUCTION

What if common sense is only local knowledge?

Part 07 follows four thinkers who did not simply add facts to the world. They changed the questions that could be asked of it.

Human intuition was shaped at ordinary speeds, visible scales, and within face-to-face social life. It is remarkably useful there. But nature is not required to remain intuitive when we examine light, atoms, formal procedures, or electronic machines. At those edges, familiar assumptions can become obstacles.

Albert Einstein challenged the idea that one universal clock and one universal space stand behind every observer. Richard Feynman helped build mathematical tools that work even when no single classical picture tells the whole story. Alan Turing separated the question “What can be calculated?” from the identity of the calculator. John von Neumann helped turn instructions into objects a machine could store and transform alongside data.

Their lesson is not that human understanding failed. It is that understanding grows when we can identify which assumption has stopped working.

These are not solitary-genius myths. Each breakthrough emerged from communities, rivals, experiments, engineers, and earlier ideas. Nor did any of the four prove that time is unreal, consciousness is an illusion, or thought is “nothing but” machinery. Their work did something subtler and more durable: it made formerly invisible premises available for examination.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE

When intuition met its limits

Across seventy years, physics and computation replaced several human-centered certainties with testable relations and formal procedures.

1887

Michelson and Morley fail to detect the expected ether drift

The experiment found no motion-dependent shift of the size predicted by a stationary light-bearing ether. Its null result became important evidence in the transformation of physics, although historians caution that Einstein’s own path to special relativity cannot be reduced to this experiment alone.

Null resultEtherExperimental evidence
1905

Einstein rebuilds space and time around invariant laws

Special relativity begins from two principles: the laws of physics take the same form in inertial frames, and light in vacuum has the same speed for inertial observers. Simultaneity, duration, and distance become frame-dependent—while the theory’s laws and spacetime interval remain invariant.

Special relativityReference frameInvariant
1915

General relativity turns gravity into spacetime geometry

Einstein’s general theory describes gravitation through curved spacetime rather than as an ordinary force acting across a fixed stage. This was a separate achievement from the 1905 special theory and required a decade of further work.

General relativityGravityCurved spacetime
1925–1927

Quantum mechanics breaks with classical trajectories

Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Born, Bohr, and others developed formalisms that predicted atomic phenomena with extraordinary accuracy. The uncertainty principle and complementarity showed why classical concepts such as a precise path cannot always be combined in one experimental description.

Quantum mechanicsUncertaintyComplementarity
1936

Turing formalizes effective calculation—and its limits

A simple abstract machine gave mathematical form to step-by-step computation. A universal machine could simulate other machines when supplied with their descriptions, while Turing’s argument also established that some well-posed problems cannot be solved by a general mechanical procedure.

Turing machineUniversalityUndecidability
1945

The stored-program design is widely circulated

The First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, written by von Neumann from work developed with the Moore School team, described a computer that could hold instructions and numerical data in memory. Its circulation helped establish a general architecture for programmable electronic computing.

EDVACStored programShared invention
1948

Feynman presents the path-integral formulation

Instead of assigning a quantum particle one classical route, the formulation calculates amplitudes by summing contributions associated with possible paths. It offered a powerful new way to formulate quantum mechanics and later quantum electrodynamics.

Path integralQuantum amplitudesFormalism
1950

Turing replaces an essence question with an imitation game

Rather than first defining “thinking,” Turing asked how a machine’s conversational performance might be judged through text. The proposal did not prove that imitation equals intelligence; it changed an unproductive question into an operational and debatable one.

Imitation gameOperational questionMachine intelligence
1956

Artificial intelligence becomes a named research field

The Dartmouth Summer Research Project gathered researchers around the proposal that features of learning and intelligence might be described precisely enough for machines to simulate them. The conjecture opened a field; it did not settle what intelligence, understanding, or consciousness are.

DartmouthArtificial intelligenceResearch program
1965

Three approaches to quantum electrodynamics are recognized

Richard Feynman shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Sin-Itiro Tomonaga and Julian Schwinger for fundamental work in quantum electrodynamics. The award is a reminder that even the most famous individual story sits inside a many-person achievement.

QEDTomonagaSchwingerFeynman

FOUR THINKERS · EIGHT EPISODES

Four assumptions placed under pressure

Each pair begins with a breakthrough, then asks what the breakthrough does—and does not—allow us to conclude.

EINSTEIN · EPISODE 01

There is no single universal “now”

Time is not an invisible clock shared by every observer

At everyday speeds, Newtonian time is an excellent approximation. Special relativity becomes necessary when relative motion and light matter. Two observers can disagree about the duration between events, the length of a moving object, or whether distant events were simultaneous—without either observer being mistaken.

This does not mean “everything is relative.” The speed of light in vacuum, the form of physical laws, and the spacetime interval provide invariants that let different observations fit one coherent account. Relativity replaced an absolute background with a more demanding structure of relationships.

CENTRAL QUESTIONIf observers disagree about time, what must remain shared for science to be possible?
SimultaneityTime dilationInvariantEvidence
EINSTEIN · EPISODE 02

Common sense belongs to a scale

An intuition can be reliable nearby and misleading at the edge of experience

Relativistic effects are not philosophical wordplay. Satellite clocks run at different rates because of both their motion and their position in Earth’s gravitational field. GPS must account for special- and general-relativistic effects to maintain useful accuracy.

The deeper lesson is methodological. We should not discard common sense everywhere; we should locate its domain. A model can be “wrong” as a universal description and still be extremely useful within a limited range. Newtonian mechanics remains indispensable even after relativity.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhich of your certainties may be accurate only because you have never left their familiar scale?
ApproximationScaleGPSModel
FEYNMAN · EPISODE 03

Prediction can advance before intuition catches up

A formalism may be reliable even when no classical picture contains it

Quantum theory resists the demand that microscopic objects always possess the familiar properties of tiny classical objects. Feynman’s path-integral formulation does not tell a simple story in which a particle secretly chooses one ordinary route. It combines amplitudes associated with alternatives and extracts probabilities from their interference.

That does not make understanding irrelevant. It separates several things we often merge: calculating an outcome, drawing a picture, explaining a mechanism, connecting concepts, and knowing when a model applies. Science can progress while these forms of understanding develop at different speeds.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhen a method predicts correctly, what further kind of understanding are you still asking for?
Path integralPredictionExplanationModel
FEYNMAN · EPISODE 04

Observation is a physical interaction

Quantum measurement does not require a conscious mind staring at a particle

In a double-slit experiment, alternatives can interfere when which-path information is unavailable. When the paths become distinguishable through a detector or interaction with the environment, interference is reduced or lost. The relevant change is physical information and entanglement—not a mystical power of human attention.

Feynman diagrams also invite a caution. They are calculational representations of terms in a theory, not literal films of tiny particles deciding what to do. A useful image can guide reasoning, but confusing representation with reality creates a new assumption in place of the old one.

CENTRAL QUESTIONHow can we use a picture without forgetting where the picture stops?
InterferenceWhich-path informationMeasurementRepresentation
TURING · EPISODE 05

Computation is a pattern, not a person

The same procedure can be carried by a clerk, symbols on paper, or a machine

Turing imagined an extremely simple worker following exact rules: read a symbol, write or erase a symbol, move one step, and enter a new state. From this austere model came a precise account of effective calculation. A universal machine could read a description of another machine and simulate it.

The achievement was paired with a boundary. There is no single algorithm that correctly decides every question about what arbitrary programs will do. Formalization did not show that “everything is computable.” It made the difference between computable and uncomputable problems visible.

CENTRAL QUESTIONDoes mechanizing a procedure explain the activity—or only specify how it can be carried out?
AlgorithmUniversal machineSimulationLimits
TURING · EPISODE 06

Ask what intelligence does

The imitation game moved debate from an invisible essence to observable performance

“Can machines think?” appears simple until every key word demands a definition. Turing proposed a conversational game as a replacement question. Its value lies partly in forcing criteria into the open: What evidence makes us attribute intelligence to anyone, human or machine?

Passing such a test would demonstrate a kind of linguistic performance under specified conditions. It would not by itself establish consciousness, emotion, truthfulness, general competence, or moral status. An operational test makes inquiry possible by narrowing a question, and that same narrowing creates blind spots.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhich human capacities are you treating as one thing when you use the word “intelligence”?
Imitation gameOperational definitionIntelligenceConsciousness
VON NEUMANN · EPISODE 07

Instructions become data

A machine can store the procedure that changes what the machine does

Early electronic computers often required laborious rewiring or external control to change tasks. In a stored-program system, instructions and data can occupy the same memory. Programs can be loaded, copied, inspected, and even modified by other programs. The machine becomes general not by containing every tool, but by reconfiguring itself through symbols.

The architecture is associated with von Neumann because his 1945 report circulated widely, yet the design emerged through collaborative work involving the EDVAC team and related projects. Names can preserve history while also compressing away contributors.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhat changes when a rule can itself become an object to be stored, copied, and transformed?
Stored programCode as dataArchitectureCollaboration
VON NEUMANN · EPISODE 08

Mechanism does not erase responsibility

Implementing thought-like procedures redistributes agency rather than settling what thought is

Electronic computers showed that some activities associated with human reasoning—calculation, search, simulation, and rule-following—could be materially implemented. Von Neumann also studied games, self-reproducing automata, and the comparison between brains and computers. These analogies opened research programs; they did not prove that minds and machines are identical.

Today’s systems join hardware, code, training data, energy, workers, institutions, and users. Calling an output “the machine’s decision” can hide this distributed arrangement. The more autonomous a system appears, the more carefully we must trace who designed its objective, supplied its world, and bears the consequences.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhen a machine acts, where should we look for the human choices inside the system?
ImplementationAgencyResponsibilityAI systems

CORE ARGUMENT

Progress begins by locating the hidden premise

The four stories differ, but each transforms an apparently natural boundary into a question that can be tested, formalized, or redesigned.

I. Intuition is adapted, not universal

Everyday experience trains us well for a narrow region of reality. It provides useful priors, not final laws. The task is to discover the conditions under which an intuition works and the point at which it becomes an error.

II. Formal systems extend thought—and expose limits

Mathematics and computation let reasoning travel where unaided imagination cannot. Yet rigor does not mean unlimited power. Relativity preserves invariants; quantum mechanics constrains possible descriptions; computability theory proves impossibilities. A formal boundary is knowledge, not failure.

III. Operational definitions reveal and conceal

A test turns an abstract question into an observable procedure. That makes comparison possible, but the procedure samples only part of the original concept. Performance is evidence about intelligence; it is not automatically evidence about every form of understanding or experience.

IV. Mechanization moves responsibility

When a task becomes executable by a machine, human judgment does not disappear. It moves into categories, objectives, interfaces, data, maintenance, and institutions. The machine’s apparent independence can make these choices harder to see precisely when they matter most.

THE CENTRAL QUESTION OF PART 07When a premise falls, what should replace it?

Removing an assumption is not the same as removing meaning. The next step is to build a better relation among evidence, explanation, formalism, and responsibility.

GLOSSARY

Words for thinking beyond intuition

Reference frame

A coordinate system and state of motion used to describe when and where events occur. Different inertial frames can assign different times and distances while remaining connected by the laws of special relativity.

Invariant

A quantity or relation that remains unchanged under a specified transformation. Relativity is built not on the claim that everything varies, but on identifying what all valid frames share.

Special and general relativity

Special relativity treats physics in inertial frames without gravity. General relativity describes gravitation through the geometry of spacetime and includes accelerating frames.

Quantum interference

The combination of probability amplitudes associated with alternative processes. Depending on their relative phases, alternatives can reinforce or cancel one another.

Which-path information

Information that can distinguish the alternatives in an interference experiment. Making paths distinguishable reduces the coherence needed for an interference pattern; a conscious observer is not required.

Path integral

A formulation of quantum mechanics associated with Feynman that calculates amplitudes through contributions from possible histories rather than one classical trajectory.

Turing machine

An abstract model of computation consisting of a tape, a read-write head, a finite set of states, and exact transition rules. Its simplicity helps define what an effective procedure can compute.

Undecidability

The property of a problem for which no algorithm can always return the correct yes-or-no answer for every valid input. It is a mathematical limit, not merely a shortage of processing power.

Imitation game

Turing’s 1950 proposal for judging a machine through text-based interaction. It operationalizes one aspect of machine intelligence but does not by itself measure consciousness or every cognitive capacity.

Stored-program computer

A computer that keeps instructions in electronic memory alongside data. This permits a general-purpose machine to change tasks by loading a different program rather than being physically rewired.

Von Neumann architecture

A family of stored-program designs with processing, memory, input, and output. The familiar name reflects von Neumann’s influential report, though the architecture grew from collaborative wartime and postwar work.

Operational definition

A definition that specifies how a concept will be observed or measured. It enables testing while always capturing only the aspects included in the operation.

A MAP OF QUESTIONS

Continue the inquiry

Which belief feels self-evident only because it fits the scale of your daily life?

Imagine changing speed, size, lifespan, culture, or position. Which part of the belief survives?

When should reliable prediction count as understanding?

Separate calculation, explanation, visualization, mechanism, control, and the ability to teach the idea.

What does a test of intelligence include—and leave out?

Choose one test, then list the capacities it samples and the capacities it cannot establish.

Which human judgment has been hidden inside a system described as automatic?

Trace its objective, categories, training examples, thresholds, interface, and lines of accountability.

What should replace an assumption after evidence breaks it?

Design a better working model without pretending that uncertainty, meaning, or responsibility has vanished.
