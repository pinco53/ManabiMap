INTRODUCTION

What happens between experience and words?

Part 05 enters the space where sound becomes a sign, private experience becomes a shared concept, and language becomes a tool for imagining what does not yet exist.

Is the inner voice the voice of thought?

Many people experience thinking as silent speech. They rehearse a conversation, name a feeling, or talk themselves through a decision. Inner speech supports working memory, self-regulation, planning, and reflection—but it is not the whole of thought. Images, emotions, bodily expectations, spatial reasoning, musical structures, and skilled action can carry intelligence without arriving first as sentences.

Language impairment also reveals a complicated picture. Some people with aphasia retain forms of nonverbal reasoning, and some can say a word internally that they cannot produce aloud. Inner speech and overt speech overlap, but neither maps neatly onto thought as a whole.

Four questions for the journey

Origins: how did human communication become open-ended language? Mind: how do words participate in memory, abstraction, and self-reflection? Diversity: how do different languages guide attention without imprisoning their speakers? AI: when a machine uses language persuasively, what would count as understanding?

Language is neither a transparent window onto thought nor a prison from which thought cannot escape. It is a living instrument that changes what a mind can notice, share, and build.

These questions are inseparable. The origin of language is also a question about the emergence of shared imagination. Linguistic diversity reveals multiple ways of organizing experience. AI, finally, forces us to ask whether meaningful behavior, embodied experience, social participation, and conscious understanding are the same thing—or only partly connected.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE

How language became a problem of mind

Philosophy, linguistics, psychology, and computation have repeatedly redrawn the boundary between words and thought.

4th century BCE

Aristotle formalizes relations among words, thought, and logic

Aristotle treated spoken sounds as signs of mental experiences and developed systematic accounts of categories, propositions, and syllogisms. Language became both an object of study and a medium for examining valid inference.

LogicCategoriesSigns
1690

Locke examines ideas, experience, and words

John Locke argued that the mind does not begin stocked with innate ideas and that knowledge grows through experience and reflection. He also warned that words can conceal confusion when speakers use them without clear ideas or shared definitions.

EmpiricismIdeasMeaning
1859–1871

Evolution reframes the origin of language

Darwin’s evolutionary account invited language to be studied as a natural capacity with a history rather than a timeless gift. Fossils cannot preserve a first sentence, so the timing and pathways of language evolution remain actively debated.

EvolutionLanguage originsContinuity
1916

Saussure describes language as a system of differences

Published after Ferdinand de Saussure’s death, the Course in General Linguistics distinguished the signifier from the signified and emphasized that linguistic values arise through relations within a system, not from natural bonds between sounds and things.

SignArbitrarinessStructure
1920s–1940s

Inner speech and linguistic relativity enter psychology

Lev Vygotsky studied how social speech can become internalized as a tool of self-guidance. Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf explored how habitual patterns of language may relate to habitual patterns of attention and interpretation.

VygotskyInner speechRelativity
1950

Turing replaces an essence with an observable test

Alan Turing proposed the imitation game as a way to discuss machine intelligence without first defining “thinking.” Linguistic interaction became a practical test of performance, though not an uncontested proof of consciousness or understanding.

Imitation gameMachine intelligenceBehavior
1957

Chomsky challenges behaviorist accounts of language

Noam Chomsky’s work on generative grammar argued that speakers possess structural knowledge that cannot be described as a list of learned sentences. His proposals transformed linguistics, while the precise nature and innateness of universal constraints remain debated.

Generative grammarSyntaxAcquisition
1980

Searle presents the Chinese Room

John Searle argued that following rules for manipulating symbols is not by itself sufficient for understanding their meaning. Critics replied that understanding might belong to the whole system, or to a system embodied and acting in a world.

Chinese RoomSyntaxSemantics
1990

The symbol-grounding problem is named

Stevan Harnad asked how the meanings of symbols can be grounded without defining every symbol only through more symbols. Perception, action, learning, and embodiment became central to debates about artificial meaning.

Symbol groundingEmbodimentReference
2017–2020s

Transformers make fluent generation scalable

The Transformer architecture enabled models that learn long-range patterns across enormous collections of language. Large and multimodal models can now generate, translate, explain, and converse—renewing old questions about competence, grounding, agency, and understanding.

TransformerLarge language modelsMultimodality

SEVEN EPISODES

Seven thresholds between sound and understanding

Each episode asks what language contributes—and what forms of mind cannot be reduced to language alone.

EPISODE 01

A world before words

When did a sound become a sign that could travel beyond the present?

The origin of language left no direct fossil. Anatomy, genetics, archaeology, comparative cognition, and the study of present-day languages offer clues, but they do not identify a single first word or date. Language probably emerged through interacting biological and cultural changes rather than one sudden invention.

Human language is remarkable for combining conventional signs into indefinitely many expressions. It can refer to absent people, imagined futures, impossible worlds, and its own grammar. Animal communication shows important continuities—signals, learning, turn-taking, even limited combinatorial patterns—so the real question is not whether other species communicate, but which capacities became unusually integrated and expandable in humans.

EPISODE 02

The discovery of the inner voice

When you think in words, who is speaking—and who is listening?

Vygotsky proposed that children’s self-directed speech can become internalized and reorganized as inner speech. What begins between people becomes a tool within a person: a way to rehearse, inhibit, plan, remember, and interpret.

But inner experience varies. Some thought is verbal, some visual or spatial, some affective or motoric. Language disorders can disrupt particular linguistic processes while leaving other forms of cognition available; inner speech itself can be selectively preserved or impaired. Language is one powerful architecture of thought, not a census of everything the mind can do.

CENTRAL QUESTIONIf thought does not always arrive as a sentence, what other forms can it take?

EPISODE 03

From instances to categories

A word can gather many different things beneath one portable idea

Calling several objects “apple” ignores differences of color, size, and history in order to preserve a useful similarity. Calling justice, gravity, or democracy by name makes an abstraction easier to hold, compare, dispute, and transmit. Words help communities stabilize concepts across people and time.

Categorization itself is not uniquely linguistic. Infants and nonhuman animals can learn categories and generalize beyond single examples. Language adds another power: it makes categories explicit, recombinable, discussable, and available for collective institutions. The label does not create every concept, but it can sharpen a boundary and change how often we notice it.

CENTRAL QUESTIONDoes naming reveal a category, construct it, or do both at once?

EPISODE 04

The power of “if”

Language lets a community inspect worlds that never happened

“If it rains tomorrow…” opens a possible future. “If I had chosen differently…” constructs a counterfactual past. Humans can plan and anticipate without speaking, and other animals show forms of prospective behavior. Language nevertheless makes hypothetical structures unusually precise, revisable, and shareable.

Formal logic turns some relations among statements into inspectable rules. Its power is real but bounded. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems concern sufficiently expressive, consistent formal systems; they do not show that all reasoning is futile or that every truth is beyond proof. They remind us that the rules of a system and the claims expressible within it must be distinguished.

CENTRAL QUESTIONWhen a conclusion follows correctly, what else must be true for us to trust it?

EPISODE 05

How languages divide color and time

Words guide attention without constructing separate physical universes

Russian distinguishes lighter goluboy from darker siniy as basic color terms. In a well-known experiment, Russian speakers were faster than English speakers at discriminating blues that crossed this lexical boundary, and verbal interference reduced the advantage. Language affected a task performed on the same visible spectrum.

Languages also spatialize time differently. Aymara speakers have been observed using a known past-in-front and unknown future-behind mapping; Mandarin can use vertical as well as horizontal temporal expressions. Such findings support linguistic relativity in a modest form: habitual language can direct attention and make some distinctions easier to recruit. It does not make translation impossible or determine everything a speaker can think.

EPISODE 06

Can AI use a word without understanding it?

Fluent output turns an old philosophical dispute into an everyday encounter

A language model learns statistical structure across sequences of tokens and uses that structure to generate continuations. This description matters, but it is not the entire behavioral story: contemporary systems can combine language with images, audio, tools, retrieval, and action. Nor does fluency alone settle questions about experience, reference, goals, or accountability.

The Chinese Room argues that formal symbol manipulation is insufficient for semantics. The systems reply asks whether understanding belongs to the complete organized system rather than one component. The robot reply asks whether perception and action could ground symbols. These are competing philosophical positions, not a laboratory result that has already decided whether any present or future AI understands.

EPISODE 07

Words as wings—and as rails

Language expands thought, while every vocabulary also inherits a point of view

Words are wings because they let a thought leave one body, cross a century, and enter another. They make promises, laws, theories, imaginary worlds, and collective plans possible. A new concept can expose a pattern that had been present but hard to see.

Words are also rails. A familiar label can make a fluid situation seem fixed; a category can hide variation; a slogan can stop inquiry. The answer is not to escape language, but to use it reflectively—to translate, rename, compare frames, notice silence, and return words to experience.

CORE ARGUMENT

Language changes thought without containing all of it

The deepest position is neither “words determine reality” nor “words merely label a finished reality.” Language and thought continually reorganize one another.

I. Thought exceeds language

Perception, emotion, motor skill, mental imagery, recognition, and many forms of anticipation need not begin as verbal propositions. Babies and nonhuman animals display rich learning before or without human language. Adults often know more than they can immediately say.

This does not make language superficial. It means the mind has multiple representational resources. A sentence can coordinate them, inspect them, and make some of them available to other people.

II. Language reorganizes attention

Learning a word can create a reliable handle for a recurring pattern. Grammatical habits can make speakers repeatedly mark time, number, evidential source, social relation, or spatial orientation. Repetition trains attention. These effects are tendencies, not walls: speakers learn new distinctions, translate, invent metaphors, and change their habits.

III. Meaning is more than a dictionary link

A word acquires meaning through contrasts with other words, relations to perception and action, histories of use, and social expectations. “Home,” “fair,” or “promise” cannot be exhausted by pointing at one object. Meaning is both grounded and public: embodied experience matters, but so do practices shared among people.

IV. AI makes “understanding” an operational question

We cannot directly inspect another mind, human or artificial. We infer understanding from flexible behavior, correction, transfer, explanation, reliable reference, participation in shared practices, and responsibility over time. No single test settles every sense of the word.

With AI, it is useful to separate at least four questions: Can the system produce appropriate language? Can it connect language reliably to the world? Can it pursue and revise goals across contexts? Does it have subjective experience? Evidence for one does not automatically answer the others.

THE CENTRAL QUESTION OF PART 05Is language the wing of thought—or its cage?

It is a wing when it gives experience a form that can travel. It becomes a cage when its inherited categories are mistaken for the only possible world.

GLOSSARY

Words for examining words

Inner speech

The experience or process of using language without overtly speaking. It may support rehearsal, planning, self-regulation, and reflection, but its vividness and frequency vary widely among people.

Arbitrariness of the sign

The absence of a necessary natural bond between most linguistic forms and what they mean. Different communities can conventionally use “dog,” “chien,” or “犬” for related concepts.

Sound symbolism and iconic signs show that arbitrariness is a powerful tendency, not an exceptionless law.

Productivity and displacement

Productivity is the capacity to create and understand new expressions from finite resources. Displacement is the capacity to communicate about things distant from the present place and time—or entirely imaginary.

Linguistic relativity

The family of proposals that differences among languages can influence habitual attention, memory, categorization, or reasoning. Evidence supports specific, context-dependent effects more strongly than the claim that language rigidly determines possible thought.

Counterfactual

A representation of what would, could, or might have happened under conditions different from actuality. Counterfactuals support explanation, regret, planning, moral judgment, and causal inference.

Chinese Room argument

John Searle’s 1980 thought experiment arguing that manipulating symbols according to rules is not sufficient for understanding meaning. Systems, robot, and virtual-mind replies challenge what the thought experiment establishes.

Symbol grounding

The problem of explaining how symbols connect to what they are about rather than deriving meaning only from relations to other symbols. Proposed resources include perception, action, embodiment, social interaction, and learned world models.

A MAP OF QUESTIONS

Continue the inquiry

What can you think clearly without putting it into words?

Try comparing a face, a route, a bodily skill, a melody, and a moral judgment.

Which word changed what you were able to notice?

Consider a scientific concept, an emotion, a social pattern, or a bias that became visible after it was named.

When does a category clarify—and when does it flatten?

Look for variation hidden inside labels such as intelligence, culture, success, failure, normal, and human.

What would persuade you that an AI system understands?

Separate linguistic skill, grounding, agency, social participation, accountability, and subjective experience.

How does speaking another language change the self?

Notice shifts in politeness, emotional distance, humor, memory, gesture, and the distinctions that feel natural to make.
