CHAPTER I

The Beginning of the Universe and Life

13.8 billion — 55 million years ago

Time, space, matter, and life all have histories. This chapter follows the long transformation from an early hot universe to a living planet where primates appeared.

13.8 billion years ago

The Big Bang

The universe began expanding from an extremely hot, dense early state and has cooled ever since. It is better understood as a change involving space itself, not as an explosion from one point into pre-existing space.

About 3 minutes after the Big Bang

Nuclei of the Light Elements

The nuclei of mostly hydrogen and helium formed. Neutral atoms appeared only about 380,000 years later; carbon, oxygen, iron, and other heavier elements would be forged much later inside stars and stellar explosions.

About 13.6 billion years ago

The First Stars and Galaxies

After the cosmic dark ages, the first stars ignited. Gravity gathered stars into early galaxies, creating the structures and chemical pathways from which later planets and life could emerge.

4.6 billion years ago

The Birth of Earth

Earth formed along with the rest of the solar system. Over immense spans of time, the young molten planet cooled and developed oceans, a crust, and continents.

4.5 billion years ago

The Birth of the Moon

The leading explanation is that a massive body struck the young Earth and the Moon formed from resulting debris. The exact conditions of the impact and the details of lunar formation remain active areas of research.

EnvironmentEnergy
At least 3.5–3.8 billion years ago

Traces of Early Life

Ancient rocks preserve chemical and geological traces that may record early life. When, where, and how life began remains unresolved, and scientists continue to debate how some of the oldest evidence should be interpreted.

About 2.7–2.4 billion years ago

Oxygenic Photosynthesis and the Great Oxidation

After oxygen-producing photosynthesis evolved and spread, atmospheric oxygen rose dramatically around 2.4 billion years ago. The origin of photosynthesis and the oxidation of the atmosphere were connected, but they were not one instantaneous event.

2.1 billion years ago

The Rise of Eukaryotes

Cells with nuclei appeared. Through endosymbiosis, ancestral cells incorporated the organisms that became mitochondria, opening a path toward more complex forms of life.

BodyEnvironment
About 580 million years ago

Complex Multicellular Life Spreads

Diverse large organisms with frond-like, ribbon-like, and quilted forms spread through Ediacaran seas. They reveal the long ecological prelude that is easily missed when the story begins with the Cambrian Explosion.

Smithsonian source ↗
About 540 million years ago

The Cambrian Explosion

Over several tens of millions of years—brief on a geological scale—the diversity of animal fossils increased greatly. Animal lineages existed earlier, and scientists still examine interacting causes involving oxygen, ecology, and developmental biology.

BodyEnvironment
430 million years ago

Life Moves onto Land

Plants, followed by arthropods, expanded from water onto land. Photosynthetic green life spread across dry ground, beginning the development of terrestrial ecosystems.

BodyEnvironmentMobility
230 million years ago

The Age of Dinosaurs

Dinosaurs diversified through the Mesozoic and became dominant in many terrestrial ecosystems. Mammals were present too, generally occupying smaller ecological roles for much of this era.

BodyEnvironment
66 million years ago

Mass Extinction and the Loss of Non-Avian Dinosaurs

A mass extinction at the K–Pg boundary, driven principally by a major asteroid impact, eliminated many groups including non-avian dinosaurs. Birds survived as one dinosaur lineage, while mammals later diversified into newly opened niches.

EnvironmentBody
55 million years ago

The Rise of Primates

Small primates adapted to life in trees. Depth perception and grasping hands became part of an evolutionary inheritance that would eventually shape the human lineage.

CHAPTER II ── HUMAN ORIGINS

CHAPTER II

Human Origins

7 million — 15 thousand years ago

Bipedalism, tools, fire, migration, symbols, and shared memory: this is not a single ladder toward modern humans, but a branching history of many human lineages.

7 million years ago

Early Hominin Candidates

Fossils such as Sahelanthropus tchadensis come from near the time when the human and chimpanzee lineages may have diverged. Their exact place in the family tree—and the date of that divergence—remain debated.

BodyEnvironment
4 million years ago

Australopithecines

Several australopithecine species lived in Africa. The famous fossil Lucy is about 3.2 million years old and combines adaptations for walking on two legs with features suited to climbing. Human evolution was a branching tree, not a straight staircase.

BodyMobilityEnvironment
About 3.3 million years ago

The Earliest Known Stone Tools

Stone tools reported from Lomekwi 3 in Kenya predate the earliest known tools associated with the genus Homo. We do not know who made them. They are the oldest evidence found so far, not necessarily the first moment any ancestor used a tool.

ToolsBodyInformation
1.8 million years ago

Beyond Africa

Early members of the genus Homo, often associated with Homo erectus, spread beyond Africa into Eurasia. Human history became a history of movement, adaptation, encounter, and repeated migration.

MobilityEnvironmentBodyCooperation
1 million years ago

The Control of Fire

Evidence for repeated fire use becomes more common over time. Fire eventually supported cooking, warmth, protection, and life after dark. Its relationship to changes in the body and brain is important but cannot be reduced to one simple cause.

ToolsEnergyBodyCooperation
300 thousand years ago

The Emergence of Homo sapiens

Fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco are among the earliest known remains assigned to our species. Rather than appearing at one point in one place, modern human traits likely developed across connected African populations over time.

BodyCooperationEnvironment
At least 100 thousand years ago

Burial and Attention to the Dead

Humans sometimes placed the dead carefully in the ground, at times with pigments or objects. Not every case proves religious ritual, but these practices show memory, care, and social relationships extending beyond death.

WorldviewCooperationInformation
70 thousand years ago

The Spread of Symbolic Behaviour

Evidence for ornaments, pigments, complex tools, and flexible behaviour accumulated in several regions. A sudden “cognitive revolution” 70,000 years ago is one interpretation, not proof that human abilities appeared everywhere at once.

WorldviewInformationCooperationTools
65 thousand years ago

A Global Human Dispersal

Populations of Homo sapiens expanded far beyond Africa, reaching Australia, Eurasia, and eventually the Americas. The movement was not one march or one route; it unfolded through many journeys, encounters, and local adaptations.

MobilityEnvironmentCooperation
About 36–21 thousand years ago

Cave Art and Ornament

The paintings of Chauvet Cave are about 36,000 years old and those at Lascaux about 21,000 years old. Evidence of ornament and visual expression is older still, so art did not suddenly begin in one cave or on one date.

ToolsInformationWorldview
40 thousand years ago

Neanderthals Disappear as a Distinct Population

Neanderthals gradually disappear from the fossil record as a distinct population. They interbred with Homo sapiens, and some people today carry Neanderthal DNA. Other human groups did not all vanish at the same moment.

BodyMobilityCooperation
About 20 thousand years ago

The Invention of Pottery

People in East Asia and elsewhere began using fired-clay containers before farming. Cooking and storage expanded the range of usable foods and changed how communities gathered around fire and managed seasonal resources.

ToolsEnergyEnvironmentCooperation
15 thousand years ago

The Domestication of Dogs

Dogs emerged from a long relationship between humans and wolves. When, where, and how domestication happened remain debated; the familiar story of friendly wolves approaching campfires is only one possible pathway.

CHAPTER III

Civilization Begins

c. 9500 BCE — 27 BCE

Farming, cities, writing, numbers, law, money, and philosophy emerged through different paths. Civilization was never one invention or one region’s story.

c. 8000 BCE

Agricultural Transformations

Cultivation, domestication, and settled life developed over long periods in Southwest Asia and several other regions. This was not one sudden switch from foraging to farming, but many processes that changed population, health, labour, and land use.

c. 9500 BCE

Göbekli Tepe

Large stone enclosures were built in what is now southeastern Türkiye. The site is crucial for studying the relationships among foraging, settlement, cultivation, and ritual, but it does not prove the simple claim that temples single-handedly created farming or civilization.

c. 7000 BCE

Settled Communities and Domestication

Villages, cultivation, and animal domestication combined at different times and by different routes. Property, specialised work, and inequality did not appear as one package; each changed over time with local environments and institutions.

From c. 6000 BCE

Irrigation and the Shared Management of Water

Communities in dry regions built channels to carry river water to fields. Irrigation supported harvests and population growth, while canal maintenance, land allocation, and salinisation created new problems of cooperation and power.

c. 4000 BCE

Uruk and Early Urbanism

Uruk became one of the earliest large cities in southern Mesopotamia, linking temples, administration, trade, and record-keeping. What counts as the “first city” depends on definitions of size and institutions, and other regions followed different paths to urban life.

Human relationships with horses deepened across the Eurasian steppe as riding, traction, and pastoralism developed over time. Horses accelerated the movement of people, goods, military power, and information between distant societies.

The Wheel and Axle

The crucial invention was not simply a round object but a working wheel-and-axle system. Combined with roads, vehicles, animals, and skilled construction, it transformed transport rather than changing the world by itself.

Alloying copper with tin and other materials produced durable tools, weapons, and ritual objects. Because ores came from distant places, metallurgy encouraged specialised labour and far-reaching trade while concentrating wealth and military power.

Iron tools and weapons became increasingly widespread in several regions. Iron did not simply replace bronze overnight; new materials, fuels, skills, and trade networks gradually changed farming, craft, and warfare.

The “Axial Age” groups major philosophical and religious changes across the first millennium BCE. The thinkers, places, and dates differ greatly; it is a comparative historical theory, not one event that happened everywhere at the same time.

The Connected Ancient and Medieval World

From c. 2nd century BCE — 1517 CE

Paper, faith, scholarship, trade, navigation, disease, and print moved across regions. Slowly but decisively, distant societies became part of increasingly connected worlds.

From c. 2nd century BCE

The Silk Roads were not one road but shifting networks of overland routes and cities across Eurasia. Silk, horses, and glass travelled alongside religions, technologies, art, and pathogens.

CHAPTER V

Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment

1492 — 1789 CE

Observation, instruments, mathematics, classification, and public argument challenged inherited authority. New claims about reason and liberty widened human possibility while coexisting with empire, slavery, and exclusion.

After 1492 CE

The Columbian Exchange

Crops, livestock, pathogens, and people moved across the Atlantic, reorganising diets and ecosystems around the world. Foods that supported population growth and diseases that devastated Indigenous societies belonged to the same historical process of contact and conquest.

MobilityEnvironmentBodyPower
16th–19th centuries CE

The Atlantic Slave Trade

More than ten million African people were forcibly transported across the Atlantic. Profits from sugar, cotton, finance, and shipping fed modern economies, while racial hierarchy and inequality were built into institutions whose effects persist.

1543 CE

Copernicus and a Moving Earth

On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres presented a mathematical system in which Earth moved around the Sun. The model did not instantly settle astronomy, but it helped open a long transformation in how evidence, calculation, and humanity’s place in the cosmos were understood.

Galileo began telescopic astronomy in 1609 and published observations of the Moon and Jupiter’s moons in 1610; observations of Venus followed. An instrument extended human sight and helped make observation a force capable of changing the accepted picture of the universe.

In the Discourse on Method, René Descartes made disciplined doubt a route toward reliable knowledge. Beginning from the act of thinking itself, his work became one foundation of modern philosophy and debates about mind, body, and certainty.

The Industrial Revolution

1769 — 1903 CE

Steam, factories, electricity, public systems, and new sciences multiplied human power and speed. The same transformation created extraordinary abundance, harsh labour, fossil-fuel dependence, and new struggles over health and rights.

James Watt patented a separate condenser that made earlier steam engines more efficient. Development and adoption took years, but steam power eventually transformed mines, factories, transport, and the scale of fossil-fuel use.

CHAPTER VII

The Twentieth Century: Science and World Wars

1893 — 1968 CE

Relativity, quantum theory, mass media, industrial war, computing, human rights, and decolonisation overturned old assumptions. Knowledge expanded human possibility—and amplified the power to control, exclude, and destroy.

1905 CE

Einstein’s Special Theory of Relativity

Albert Einstein showed that measurements of time and space depend on relative motion while the speed of light remains constant. The familiar idea of one absolute time gave way to spacetime relations that later became essential to modern physics and technology.

Industrial synthesis turned atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia at large scale. Fertiliser supported food production and population growth, while the same chemistry served warfare and increased fossil-fuel use, water pollution, and disruption of the nitrogen cycle.

After long movements in many countries, women gained voting rights and wider political participation. Timing and inclusion differed sharply, and exclusions based on race, class, and colonial status continued after formal suffrage.
